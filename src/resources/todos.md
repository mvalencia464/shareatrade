**Approach it as a genuine public utility first, with your services as an optional, opt-in layer that only appears when it’s clearly helpful—never as the point of the site.**

The post-fire reality in Spokane/Inland Northwest makes this especially powerful if you lean into utility and trust instead of lead-gen energy. People are already stressed, dealing with insurance, contractors who are suddenly overwhelmed, and a flood of opportunistic marketing. A clean, useful directory that just _works_ will stand out precisely because it doesn’t feel like another pitch.

### Core positioning that stays tasteful

Position the site as:

> “A free, community-maintained directory of local contractors so Spokane homeowners can quickly share reliable options in Facebook groups and neighborhood chats—especially useful right now while people are rebuilding.”

That framing does three things:

- Makes the primary value clear to homeowners (easy sharing + organized social proof).
- Signals to contractors that the directory itself is the benefit (visibility + shareable profile), not a funnel.
- Leaves room for your services without making them the reason the site exists.

### How to soft-offer your services without killing the resource feel

**1. Keep the primary pages pure.**

Directory search results, individual contractor profiles, and the “Get listed” flow should stay focused on the contractor’s info, reviews, socials, website, and contact details. No service pitch in the main content area or near the phone/email buttons.

**2. Place the soft CTA in low-pressure, contextual spots only:**

- On the contractor’s own profile (after the useful info): a small, quiet footer or side note like
  _“Need help getting more Google reviews, cleaning up your Business Profile, or a simple website that matches this listing? Reach out—happy to help other local contractors.”_
  Link to a short contact form or your email. Keep the language peer-to-peer, not salesy.
- On the “Get listed” page (for contractors adding themselves): after they submit, or in a small note, offer the same optional help. Make it clear the listing is free either way.
- In the site footer or a discreet “For contractors” link in the nav: a short page that explains the free directory benefit and, secondary, the paid services. Title it something like “Resources for contractors” rather than “Hire me.”

**3. Language rules that keep it from feeling tacky:**

- Speak as a fellow local who built a tool, not as a vendor hunting clients.
- Use “if you ever want help with…” or “happy to help other local contractors with…” instead of “we offer packages” or “boost your reviews now.”
- Never put the soft CTA next to the “View profile / Call / Email” actions. Those should stay clean.
- Avoid countdown timers, “limited spots,” or urgency language. Post-fire is already urgent enough.

**4. Make the free directory actually valuable on its own.**

Contractors should feel the listing helps them even if they never talk to you. Good shareable links, clean mobile view, visible social icons, Google review counts pulled in, and easy “copy share text” buttons matter more than any upsell. When homeowners paste the link in a Facebook group, the contractor looks professional and the directory looks useful. That builds goodwill that later makes a soft offer feel natural rather than extractive.

### Homeowner insurance / rebuilding resource

This is a strong complementary piece if done carefully. Frame it as practical orientation, not legal advice:

- Short, clear pages or a simple guide: what to document before and after work starts, common insurance pitfalls people are running into locally, questions to ask adjusters, how to get multiple contractor bids that are comparable, red flags, and links to official resources (WA insurance commissioner, FEMA if relevant, local rebuild programs, etc.).
- Keep the tone calm and factual. “Here’s what many homeowners are finding useful right now” lands better than fear-based language.
- You can lightly note at the bottom that the directory exists to help people find contractors when they’re ready, and that clean Google profiles + websites make the insurance/contractor conversation smoother. Again, optional and secondary.

This resource also gives the whole site a clear “post-fire helpful” reason to exist beyond pure lead gen, which strengthens the non-tacky positioning.

### Practical rollout sequence that protects the tone

1. Make the free directory and share experience excellent first.
2. Soft-launch the contractor help note on profiles and the get-listed page using the peer language above.
3. Add the homeowner rebuild/insurance orientation when you have solid, accurate local info.
4. Only later (if at all) consider a separate “services” page. Most of the value will come from people discovering the soft offer while using something that already helped them.

The site already has the right bones—clean profiles with socials, reviews, contact info, and shareable links. Protect that utility hard. Treat your services as an optional help desk for the people who are already benefiting from the free tool. When the primary experience feels generous and local, the secondary offer stops feeling like a pitch and starts feeling like a natural next step for the contractors who want it.
